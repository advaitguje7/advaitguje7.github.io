Piezo Pickup: five-stage BJT/MOSFET transistor audio amplifier

Specifications: To design an audio amplifier which delivers an average power of 0.1 W to an 8-ohm speaker from a piezoelectric transducer that produces a 100 mV peak sinusoidal signal.

1. Design Overview

This project combines my passion for music and analog circuit design. I designed it around an acoustic guitar, but I aim to improve the design to allow it to be used for anyone's preferred acoustic instrument.

As the name suggests, the pickup uses a piezoelectric sensor/transducer to capture vibrations off the body of an instrument. After placing the transducer stage, the next logical step would be to add a buffer stage to reduce loading effects of the piezoelectric pickup. However, a piezo is not your average input.

A piezoelectric transducer uses the piezoelectric effect, which means it accumulates an electric charge when stressed mechanically. This effect causes it to behave more like a voltage source with a small capacitor in series. If a circuit with a low resistance is added, the circuit behaves like a high-pass RC filter with a high cutoff frequency. Therefore, low-band frequencies are attenuated, and the output signal sounds thin. (You can hear this when you're connecting a piezo directly to an audio power amplifier, such as an LM386).


We can add a source-follower n-channel MOSFET circuit (which has a very high input impedance) to account for this. The high input impedance reduces the high-pass cutoff frequency, allowing most of the signal to pass through unaffected. A low-pass RC filter is then placed at the output to limit the frequency range to below 2kHz, which is the most I need my acoustic guitar to output. The high-pass filters (capacitors) between stages limit the lower-bound of the frequency range to 80Hz.

The low-pass filter leads into a another buffer, this time an emitter-follower, that reduces the loading effect before we actually amplify the signal in the gain stage. The gain stage comprises two common-emitters designed with gains of 3.3 and 4 respectively. It drives the final emitter-follower, which provides current gain and lowers the output impedance (again) to drive an 8-ohm speaker at 100mW. (Yes, it is low, but that's class A amplifiers for you. Any higher would send the transistors into cutoff or saturation).




2. Simulation

Once the circuit is designed, I simulated the output as a sine wave with an amplitude of 100mV. I decided on this value after attaching a piezo with a 1M resistor across the leads onto my guitar. After a couple of strums, I got with an average peak-to-peak value of 100mV. You can see the resulting transient and ac analyses below, which show a voltage gain of approximately 10 (20dB).

A note: the MOSFET buffer lowers the input amplitude slightly since a source-follower circuit has a gain of less than 1.


3. Prototyping

Wireless Gamepad: bluetooth video game controller based in C++ using an ESP-32

1. Overview

This device is a bluetooth video game controller that runs on an ESP-32 in C++. The project was made so that I could play Minecraft on my phone more comfortably. The device has a modular HAL-architecture that the user can modify as per their needs. The goal was to re-create a game controller as accurately as possible. The gamepad is currently only compatible/usable with iOS.

2. Motivation

This project's idea began over my sophomore year winter break due to frustrations with Minecraft's pocket edition interface. I was about to take ECE 2564: Embedded Systems, so this felt like the perfect opportunity to build my own controller and implement the concepts I learned in the course.

V1 was a purely experimental endeavor with no regard for planning, design, or forethought. I just cloned a BLE library and mapped switches to various inputs. I started with basic strafing using four pushbuttons, then replaced the buttons with a joystick. I then added another joystick, triggers, and a D-pad. While I got it to work, it was hardly robust or easy to debug.

I then showed it to my professor, who gave me guidance on how to improve it. As she put it so eloquently, "This code would get a failing grade if this was submitted in my class."

I implemented her advice in V2.

3. Hardware

Figure 3: Final Prototype Iteration (V2) Parts:

  • ESP-WROOM-32 (x1)
  • Tactile Pushbuttons (x8) [ABXY, D-Pad]
  • Joysticks (x2)
  • Horizontal Momentary Switches (x2) [Triggers]
  • Horizontal Tactile Switches (x2) [LB, RB]
The PCB is a 4-layer board. The design can be made with a 2-layer design quite easily, as per the Espressif’s technical documentation, but I wanted to try my hand at a more complicated design. I created custom footprints for the LB, RB, LT, and RT inputs to allow for horizontal switches to be soldered.

4. Software

The software was developed in C++ using a modular architecture designed to separate hardware-specific functionality from application logic. All inputs are non-blocking, as in they are sampled once a cycle within the main loop, allowing the controller to remain responsive while simultaneously handling button processing, joystick updates, and Bluetooth communication.

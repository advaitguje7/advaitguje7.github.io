(2) Piezo Pickup
five-stage bipolar/mosfet transistor audio amplifier with passive filtering and a piezo transducer

Specifications: To design an audio amplifier which delivers an average power of 0.1 W to an 8-ohm speaker from a piezoelectric transducer that produces a 100 mV peak sinusoidal signal.

Design Overview

This project combines my passion for music and analog circuit design. I designed it around an acoustic guitar, but I aim to improve the design to allow it to be used for anyone's preferred acoustic instrument.

As the name suggests, the pickup uses a piezoelectric sensor/transducer to capture vibrations off the body of an instrument. After placing the transducer stage, the next logical step would be to add a buffer stage to reduce loading effects of the piezoelectric pickup. However, a piezo is not your average input.

A piezoelectric transducer uses the piezoelectric effect, which means it accumulates an electric charge when stressed mechanically. This effect causes it to behave more like a voltage source with a small capacitor in series. If a circuit with a low resistance is added, the circuit behaves like a high-pass RC filter with a high cutoff frequency. Therefore, low-band frequencies are attenuated, and the output signal sounds thin. (You can hear this when your'e connecting a piezo directly to an audio power amplifier, such as an LM386).


We can add a source-follower n-channel MOSFET circuit (which has a very high input impedance) to account for this. The high input impedance reduces the high-pass cutoff frequency, allowing most of the signal to pass through unaffected. A low-pass RC filter is then placed at the output to limit the frequency range to below 2kHz, which is the most I want need my acoustic guitar to output. The high-pass filters (capacitors) between stages limit the lower-bound of the frequency range to 80Hz.

The low-pass filter leads into a another buffer, this time an emitter-follower, that reduces the loading effect before we actually amplify the signal in the gain stage. The gain stage comprises two common-emitters designed with gains of 3.3 and 4 respectively. It drives the final emitter-follower, which provides current gain and lowers the output impedance (again) to drive an 8-ohm speaker at 100mW. (Yes, it is low, but that's class A amplifiers for you. Any higher would send the transistors into cutoff or saturation).




Simulation

Once the circuit is designed, I simulated the output as a sine wave with an amplitude of 100mV. I decided on this value after attaching a piezo with a 1M resistor across the leads onto my guitar. After a couple of strums, I got with an average peak-to-peak value of 100mV. You can see the resulting transient and ac analyses below, which show a voltage gain of approximately 10 (20dB).

A note: the MOSFET buffer lowers the input amplitude slightly since a source-follower circuit has a gain of less than 1.

(3) TablaGestures - Github
rhythm interface based in OpenCV and Mediapipe to count through a taal on your hands

How does Indian Classical Rhythm Work?

Indian Classical percussion is based on the taal, or a rhythm cycle, upon which the melody is improvised. Each "note" produced by a percussive instrument (such as a tabla, dholak, or mridangam) is assigned a distinctive syllable, not dissimilar to solfege (or as we'd say, sargam). As an example, the first taal a student learns is Teentaal, which is a 16-beat cycle with accents on the first, fifth, and thirteenth beats, as shown below:

Dha Dhin Dhin Dha | Dha Dhin Dhin Dha | Dha Tin Tin Ta | Ta Dhin Dhin Dha

We can count through a cycle using the joints and tips of the fingers on our hand. TablaGestures is a program based in Python using MediaPipe in OpenCV to visualize these beats.
